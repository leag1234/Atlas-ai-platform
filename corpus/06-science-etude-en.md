# Study summary — Effect of sleep duration on cognitive performance in adults

## Background

Previous research has suggested a link between insufficient sleep and reduced cognitive
performance, but many earlier studies relied on self-reported sleep duration, which is
known to be unreliable. This study used objective measurement to re-examine the association.

## Methods

The study followed 312 healthy adults aged between 25 and 55 over a period of eight weeks.
Sleep duration was measured objectively using wrist actigraphy. Cognitive performance was
assessed weekly through a standardised battery of tests covering attention, working memory
and reaction time. Participants were divided into three groups: short sleepers (less than
six hours), normal sleepers (six to eight hours), and long sleepers (more than eight hours).

## Results

Normal sleepers scored on average **14 percent higher** on working memory tasks than short
sleepers. Reaction time in the short-sleeper group was 8 percent slower. Interestingly, long
sleepers did not outperform normal sleepers; their scores were statistically indistinguishable
from the normal group. The association between short sleep and reduced performance was
strongest for attention tasks.

## Limitations

The study has several limitations. The sample was drawn from a single geographic region and
consisted mainly of office workers, which limits generalisability. The eight-week duration
may be too short to capture long-term effects. Actigraphy measures movement rather than sleep
directly, and may overestimate sleep duration. The study is observational and therefore
cannot establish causation.

## Conclusion

The findings are consistent with the hypothesis that sleeping fewer than six hours is
associated with measurably lower cognitive performance, particularly for attention and
working memory. The authors stress that this is an association and that a randomised
controlled trial would be needed to confirm a causal relationship. No funding conflict was
declared.
